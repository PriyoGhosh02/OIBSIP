About Albert Einstein

Albert Einstein was born on March 14, 1879, in Ulm, in the Kingdom of Württemberg of the German Empire. From an early age, he exhibited an extraordinary curiosity regarding natural phenomena. When his father introduced him to a magnetic pocket compass at the age of five, Einstein became captivated by the invisible physical forces directing the needle, fostering a lifelong fascination with the hidden order governing space and nature.

He pursued his formal higher education at the Swiss Federal Polytechnic School in Zurich, receiving his academic teaching diploma in 1900. After encountering difficulties obtaining an academic appointment, Einstein joined the Federal Patent Office in Bern as a patent examiner. During this intellectually fertile period, he independently completed his 1905 “Annus Mirabilis” (Miracle Year) papers, introducing the special theory of relativity, formulating the mass-energy equivalence principle (E = mc²), and explaining the photoelectric effect.

In 1915, after nearly a decade of rigorous mathematical refinement, Einstein completed the general theory of relativity. His groundbreaking framework proposed that gravitation is not an instantaneous force acting between masses, but rather the geometric curvature of spacetime induced by mass and energy. The dramatic observational confirmation of this theory during the solar eclipse of May 1919 brought Einstein instantaneous international renown and elevated him to the foremost icon of scientific genius.

Einstein was awarded the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics for his foundational explanation of the photoelectric effect, which played an indispensable role in the genesis of quantum mechanics. Confronting the rising persecution of Jewish scholars in Germany during the 1930s, he renounced his German citizenship and immigrated to the United States in 1933, accepting a lifetime professorship at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, New Jersey. Throughout his subsequent life, he remained an outspoken global advocate for international pacifism, human rights, and social justice until his death in 1955.

Scientific Contributions

Special Relativity

Introduced a new understanding of space and time, demonstrating that the laws of physics and the speed of light are identical for all observers in uniform motion, leading to the renowned equation E = mc².

General Relativity

Described gravity as the curvature of four-dimensional spacetime caused by mass and energy, fundamentally transforming cosmology and predicting gravitational waves, black holes, and the bending of starlight.

Photoelectric Effect

Demonstrated that light behaves as discrete packets of energy called quanta (photons). This breakthrough explained how light ejects electrons from matter and established the foundation of quantum physics, earning him the 1921 Nobel Prize.
